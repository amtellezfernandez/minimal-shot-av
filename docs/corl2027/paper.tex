\begin{abstract}
We study a failure mode that is easy to miss in closed-loop imitation learning:
policies can be well calibrated inside their training simulator while selecting
geometrically invalid actions after transfer through a different observation and dynamics
interface. The controlled setting is a discrete autonomous-driving token library with a
geometry-driven expert, Spotlight Reflex. Internally, plain imitation does not recover the
expert mechanism: on a 1080-rollout held-out Latin-hypercube generator sweep,
Continuous-BC, Token-BC, and Token-RNN-BC reach only 77--78\% pass rate with
3.0--4.3\% Proximity Violations. Two iterations of DAgger-style on-policy relabeling
nearly closes this internal frontier, reaching 94.54\% pass and 0.19\% PV versus
Spotlight Reflex at 94.44\% pass and 0.65\% PV. But the same learned policy does not
transfer cleanly to Waymo AlpaSim through a front-camera proxy adapter: clamping lateral
magnitude removes some failures but leaves low progress, and a hard geometric suppression
layer vetoes the DAgger argmax on 395/398 frames (99.25\%), collapsing the hybrid policy
almost entirely back to Spotlight-style selection. The result is not a new winning method;
it is a diagnostic conclusion: offline accuracy and internal closed-loop success can be
misleading proxies for external robustness when learned token calibration is coupled to the
training simulator's geometry and state reconstruction.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{autonomous driving, imitation learning, DAgger, simulator transfer,
discrete action space, calibration, ManeuverToken, geometric world-state}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================

Long-tail failure is a central open problem in autonomous driving. Modern end-to-end
policies achieve near-human performance on the common driving distribution but degrade
sharply on the rare scenario compositions that matter most: wrong-way actors, construction
corridors, multi-hazard interactions~\citep{dauner2023parting,hu2023planning}. The dominant
response is scale—more data, more modalities, larger
models~\citep{chitta2022transfuser,brohan2023rt2}. We pursue an orthogonal direction:
\emph{reducing} action-space expressiveness as a structural regularizer.

The argument is topological rather than architectural. In a multi-hazard scene, the safe
trajectory set can split into disconnected components: for example, pass left, pass right,
or stop before the conflict. A continuous regressor moving between components can produce an
intermediate trajectory that belongs to neither component and intersects the unsafe set. A
discrete selector avoids this interpolation path by choosing among maneuver representatives
with explicit clearance margins. Policy uncertainty changes which representative is chosen;
it does not create a new trajectory between representatives.

We make four contributions (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}):

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Non-interpolation theorem (Sec.~\ref{sec:method:topology}).}
    We state a conditional safety result: if tokens lie inside disconnected safe maneuver
    classes with clearance margin greater than execution noise, discrete selection cannot
    enter the unsafe set by interpolation, while continuous transitions between classes must
    cross it.

  \item \textbf{Geometric World-State Encoding (Sec.~\ref{sec:method:wstate}).}
    Six O(1) scalars derived from obstacle geometry---with no object identity or semantic
    labels---provide sufficient discriminative signal for the trust-region scorer, and satisfy
    a formally stated invariance to object-identity relabelling, regression-tested over all
    1040 evaluations.

  \item \textbf{Held-out generator evidence with strong learned baselines
    (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp:lhs}).}
    Fixed-suite evaluations show that plain BC fails even with the same tokens and state
    features. A held-out Latin-hypercube generator sweep then shows the sharper frontier:
    two-step DAgger matches Spotlight Reflex on pass rate and improves PV rate, while
    one-step DAgger is weaker, three-step DAgger regresses, and trajectory scorers over
    token candidates do not close the stress-suite gap.

  \item \textbf{External transfer falsification in AlpaSim
    (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp:alpasim}).}
    The internally strong DAgger policy fails to preserve its calibration through a
    sensor-realistic proxy-state adapter. Hard geometric suppression vetoes nearly every
    DAgger argmax, showing that the learned token selector and the external geometry
    interface occupy incompatible decision spaces.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Long-tail failure in imitation learning.}
\citet{pomerleau1989alvinn} and \citet{ross2011dagger} established covariate shift as the
core failure mode of behavior cloning. \citet{ho2016gail} mitigated it adversarially but
retained a continuous action space. \citet{bansal2018chauffeurnet} synthesised worst-case
training scenarios; \citet{dauner2023parting} showed that rule-based planners outperform
learned ones on nuPlan~\citep{caesar2021nuplan} in many regimes—supporting the view that
structural inductive biases matter more than model scale for long-tail coverage.

\paragraph{Discrete action spaces.}
DQN~\citep{mnih2015human} demonstrated that discretizing the action space enables stable
deep RL where continuous policy gradients fail. Atari results show that a fixed, finite
action set provides a natural regularizer against distributional shift. We apply this
structural idea to the planning setting without RL: ManeuverTokens provide a finite,
semantically grounded action set whose footprints are invariant to scene uncertainty.

\paragraph{Potential-field and reactive planners.}
Potential-field methods~\citep{khatib1986potential} combine attractive (goal) and repulsive
(obstacle) forces to produce continuous navigation directions. Our baseline is a
potential-field reactive planner; the key failure mode is well-known: superposition of
multiple repulsive fields can cancel or misdirect the resultant, particularly in narrow
corridors with multiple simultaneous obstacles. ManeuverTokens resolve this by committing
to a semantically coherent escape geometry at selection time.

\paragraph{Geometric abstraction for planning.}
Conditional imitation learning~\citep{codevilla2018end} uses a route command to condition
a continuous policy. Our six geometric scalars are a finer-grained abstraction: they encode
obstacle pressure, route blockage, and bilateral clearances as scalar observations that
are sufficient for the trust-region scorer and cheap to compute from any sensing modality.

\paragraph{Simulator transfer and validation scope.}
\citet{khurana2022differentiable} and \citet{shi2024motiondiffuser} study trajectory
prediction in sensor-realistic environments. Our AlpaSim adapter is a transfer diagnostic
that decouples policy logic from sensor processing; it is not a substitute for physics
simulation or real-vehicle validation.

\subsection{Discrete ManeuverToken Space}
\label{sec:method:tokens}

We define nine ManeuverTokens (Table~\ref{tab:tokens}) covering the evaluated
safety-critical response classes of the SE(2) maneuver manifold. Each token is a pair
$(\alpha, \delta)$: a speed scale
$\alpha \in [0,1]$ relative to current ego speed and a signed lateral offset $\delta \in
\mathbb{R}$ (m), realized as a 20-waypoint, 5-second trajectory at 4~Hz via cubic spline.

\paragraph{Coverage for the evaluated hazard family.}
The nine tokens are not claimed to be globally optimal. They are a minimal engineering
instantiation for the hazard family evaluated here: hard stop, cautious forward progress,
nominal progress, yield-without-stopping, bilateral mild avoidance, bilateral strong
avoidance, and post-avoidance recovery.

\begin{proposition}[Token removal induces a maneuver gap]
For the evaluated multi-hazard family, removing any one response class from the 9-token
library eliminates at least one feasible safe response mode: longitudinal hard stop,
cautious progress, mild lateral avoidance, strong lateral avoidance, or recovery. The
resulting controller must either overreact (e.g., full evasion for a mild obstacle),
underreact (e.g., nudge where full evasion is required), or fail to re-enter the lane.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Geometry-Invariance]
Let $T$ be any bijection on object labels leaving obstacle positions unchanged. Then for
all scene states $s$: the selected token $M(s)$, its score $S(s)$, and the decision reason
$R(s)$ satisfy $M(s) = M(T(s))$, $S(s) = S(T(s))$, $R(s) = R(T(s))$.
\end{proposition}

The geometry-invariance proposition follows from the encoding in
Sec.~\ref{sec:method:wstate}: no token score depends on object labels. We regression-test
it over all 1040 evaluations by relabelling all obstacles in every scene and asserting
equality of outputs.

\subsection{Topological Safety Through Non-Interpolation}
\label{sec:method:topology}

Let $\mathcal{A}$ denote the action space of 5\,s trajectories, and for scene state $s$
let $\mathcal{U}(s) \subset \mathcal{A}$ be the unsafe set (trajectories violating the
clearance constraint). Let $\mathcal{S}(s)=\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{U}(s)$ be the safe
set. In multi-hazard scenes, $\mathcal{S}(s)$ may decompose into disjoint maneuver classes
$\mathcal{S}_1(s),\ldots,\mathcal{S}_K(s)$, such as stop-before-conflict, pass-left, and
pass-right.

We use the trajectory metric
$d(\tau_a,\tau_b)=\max_t \|\tau_a(t)-\tau_b(t)\|_2$ over the 5\,s horizon. A continuous
policy outputs $a_c(s)\in\mathcal{A}$ and is executed with bounded decision or actuation
error $\epsilon$, $\|\epsilon\|\le\eta$. A token policy selects
$\tau_i\in T=\{\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_N\}$ and executes $\tilde{\tau}_i=\tau_i+\epsilon$.

\begin{theorem}[Safety through non-interpolation]
\label{thm:noninterp}
Fix a scene state $s$. Suppose:
\begin{enumerate}
\item each selected token $\tau_i$ lies in a safe maneuver class $\mathcal{S}_k(s)$;
\item each selected token has clearance margin
  $\mathrm{dist}(\tau_i,\mathcal{U}(s))>\eta$;
\item there exist two safe maneuver classes $\mathcal{S}_i(s)$ and $\mathcal{S}_j(s)$ such
  that every continuous path in $\mathcal{A}$ from $\mathcal{S}_i(s)$ to
  $\mathcal{S}_j(s)$ intersects $\mathcal{U}(s)$.
\end{enumerate}
Then every noisy execution of a selected token remains safe, while any continuous
controller that changes between $\mathcal{S}_i(s)$ and $\mathcal{S}_j(s)$ by interpolation
must pass through an unsafe trajectory.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For a token, $\mathrm{dist}(\tau_i,\mathcal{U}(s))>\eta$ implies that every perturbation
with norm at most $\eta$ remains outside $\mathcal{U}(s)$. For the continuous case, let
$\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal{A}$ be any continuous transition from a trajectory in
$\mathcal{S}_i(s)$ to one in $\mathcal{S}_j(s)$. By assumption, $\gamma$ intersects
$\mathcal{U}(s)$, so there exists $t^\star$ with $\gamma(t^\star)\in\mathcal{U}(s)$.
Thus interpolation between disconnected safe maneuver classes necessarily traverses the
unsafe set, whereas token selection jumps between representatives and does not execute the
intermediate path.
\end{proof}

The theorem is conditional: it does not prove that nine tokens are globally optimal, nor
that the specific numeric offsets in Table~\ref{tab:tokens} are unique. It identifies the
property we test empirically: safety comes from selecting margin-separated maneuver
classes, not from continuous interpolation through the action manifold.

\subsection{Geometric World-State Encoding}
\label{sec:method:wstate}

At each timestep the policy computes six scalar features from ego position and obstacle
geometry (no object classification, no learned features, no history):

\begin{align}
\scalar{pressure}    &= \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{10 - d_{\min}}{10}, 0, 1\right)
    \label{eq:pressure} \\
\scalar{blockage}    &\in [0,1], \quad \text{fraction of forward corridor cross-section occupied}
    \label{eq:blockage} \\
\scalar{blocked}     &\in \{0,1\}, \quad \text{hard stop within braking distance}
    \label{eq:blocked} \\
\scalar{left\_clr}   &= \text{free lateral space left at half vehicle width (m)}
    \label{eq:lclr} \\
\scalar{right\_clr}  &= \text{free lateral space right at half vehicle width (m)}
    \label{eq:rclr} \\
\scalar{escape}      &= \arg\max(\scalar{left\_clr},\;\scalar{right\_clr})
    \label{eq:escape}
\end{align}

All six scalars are $O(1)$ given a sorted obstacle list ($d_\text{min}$ by distance).
The encoding contains no object identity or semantic labels, so geometry-invariance follows
directly. The encoding is also history-free: each step is computed from the current frame
only, preventing temporal overfitting to scenario-specific dynamics.

\subsection{Trust-Region Scoring}
\label{sec:method:scoring}

Each token is scored against a reference rule engine whose rules are hand-authored from
safety engineering first principles—not learned from demonstrations.

\paragraph{Speed-scaled tolerance.}
\begin{equation}
s(v) = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{v - 1.4}{11.0 - 1.4},\; 0,\; 1\right) \times 0.5 + 0.5
\end{equation}
$s(v) \in [0.5, 1.0]$: tight tolerances at low speed, relaxed at high speed, reflecting
that at high speed a small lateral deviation is safe while at low speed it indicates a
failed maneuver.

\paragraph{Trust-region thresholds.} At 3~s horizon: $\pm 1.0 \cdot s(v)$~m lateral,
$\pm 4.0 \cdot s(v)$~m longitudinal. At 5~s: $\pm 1.8 \cdot s(v)$~m, $\pm 7.2 \cdot s(v)$~m.

\paragraph{Token score.} Let $\delta$ be the normalized overshoot beyond the trust region,
and $r$ a reference rule with target score $r.\texttt{score} \in [0, 100]$:
\begin{equation}
\text{score}_t(\tau, r) = \begin{cases} r.\texttt{score} & \delta = 0 \\ \max(r.\texttt{score}
\cdot 0.1^{\delta},\; 4.0) & \delta > 0 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
S(\tau) = 0.5 \cdot \max_r \text{score}_{3\text{s}}(\tau, r) + 0.5 \cdot \max_r
\text{score}_{5\text{s}}(\tau, r) + P(\tau)
\end{equation}
where $P(\tau)$ adds clearance penalties ($-1000$ if clearance $< 0.55$~m) and progress
bonuses (+40 max). The token $\tau^* = \arg\max_\tau S(\tau)$ is executed.

\paragraph{Reference rules.}
Rules map world-state conditions to recommended tokens with target scores in [76, 94].
Key rules: \scalar{pressure}~$< 0.25$ and open corridor $\to$ \token{maintain}~(92);
\scalar{pressure}~$> 0.20$ with preferred escape $\to$ \token{nudge}$_{\text{escape}}$~(91);
\scalar{pressure}~$\ge 0.35$ with preferred escape $\to$
\token{evasive}$_{\text{escape}}$~(94);
\scalar{blocked}~$= 1 \to$ \token{stop}~(82). Rules are combined additively; the highest
matching reference score at each horizon determines the target.

\subsection{AlpaSim Proxy-State Adapter}
\label{sec:method:alpasim}

Waymo's AlpaSim provides sensor-realistic front-camera input, route command, and ego
dynamics. We bridge these observations to the same
geometric scalar interface via four channels:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Route $\to$ lane curvature.} LEFT/STRAIGHT/RIGHT generates a 5-point sigmoid
  spline ($\pm 2.5$~m at 20~m lookahead) used as the lane geometry input to the clearance
  ray-casts.

\item \textbf{Brightness $\to$ visibility augmentation.}
  $\text{vis} = \max(0,(0.35 - \mu_I)/0.35)$, where $\mu_I$ is mean frame brightness.
  Below 35\% brightness, \scalar{pressure} is augmented proportionally.

\item \textbf{Ego dynamics $\to$ risk scalars.}
  $\text{brake} = \max(0,-a/4.0)$; $\text{low\_speed} = \max(0,(1.5-v)/1.5)$.
  These augment the reference rule thresholds.

\item \textbf{Structured hazards $\to$ obstacle primitives.}
  Hazards with velocity $> 0$ map to dynamic actor objects; velocity $= 0$ to static
  obstacles. Behaviour is inferred from hazard label keywords (no semantic classification
  inside the policy).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Simulation Benchmark}
\label{sec:exp:sim}

\paragraph{Setup.}
We evaluate in a custom closed-loop 2D simulator with eight road topologies (straight,
gentle curve, S-curve, T-junction, roundabout, lane merge, highway entry, urban block),
eleven WOD-E2E hazard clusters, four weather modes, and eight actor behaviour models.
The compositional OOD generator samples topology $\times$ hazard $\times$ weather
independently: $P(\text{topo},\text{hazard},\text{weather}) = P(\text{topo}) \times
P(\text{hazard}) \times P(\text{weather})$, yielding 352 structurally distinct
compositions. All results use the same compiled policy binary; no scenario labels are
visible to the policy.

\paragraph{Baseline.}
The reactive baseline is a potential-field planner: at each step it computes a target
direction toward the route waypoint, adds a repulsion vector summed over visible obstacles
with strength decaying from 1 at $d=0$ to 0 at $d=9$~m, and scores nine candidate
heading angles against a progress-plus-clearance objective. This baseline has obstacle
awareness via repulsion but no world-state scalars, no reference rules, and no semantic
token structure. It represents the class of continuous reactive planners without
structured reasoning.

\paragraph{Safety metric definition.}
We define a \textbf{Proximity Violation} as any evaluation in which the rollout does not
reach the goal \emph{and} at least one step has
$C > 0.90$, where $C = \max(0, \min(1, (5 - d_\text{min}) / 5))$
and $d_\text{min}$ is the minimum signed clearance (metres) between the ego-vehicle centre
and any visible obstacle surface.
$C > 0.90$ corresponds to $d_\text{min} < 0.25$~m.
The metric is proximity-based rather than fault-based: it does not distinguish whether the
ego vehicle moved into an obstacle or an obstacle moved into the ego vehicle.
However, the simulator's actor behaviour models (crossing, cut-in, head-on approach,
sudden braking) do not include rear-approach scenarios; every proximity violation
therefore represents a failure of the ego policy to maintain safe separation,
not an unavoidable passive impact.
Rollouts that \emph{reach the goal} with momentarily high $C$ (close pass) are not
flagged; only failed rollouts with proximity violations count.

This supports the non-interpolation mechanism in Theorem~\ref{thm:noninterp}: gradient
descent on the same features approximates the escape rule, but does not enforce the
margin-separated maneuver class.

\subsection{Held-Out Latin-Hypercube Sweep}
\label{sec:exp:lhs}

The fixed suites above are useful diagnostics, but they are hand-authored stress tests.
For the primary OOD result we sample 12 held-out generator profiles with Latin-hypercube
coverage over nine scenario axes: lane-width range, gauntlet pressure, adversarial
pressure, hidden-suite pressure, visibility, actor intrusion scale, ambient obstacle
count, and hidden hazard count. Each profile is evaluated on matched seeds 1--10 across
Gauntlet, Adversarial, and Hidden suites, for 1080 closed-loop rollouts per agent.

The result is a falsification of the stronger methods claim. The initial soft hybrid

\subsection{What the Strong Baselines Change}
\label{sec:analysis:discrete}

The BC comparison in Table~\ref{tab:bc_comparison} isolates what the token vocabulary
does \emph{not} provide. Both Token-BC and Continuous-BC operate on the same 10 geometric
features as Spotlight Reflex. Token-BC even uses the identical 9-token vocabulary. Yet
both BC agents produce PVs on 3.8--4.4\% of Gauntlet scenarios while Spotlight has none
observed. Table~\ref{tab:lhs_main} then shows that recurrent memory alone is not enough:
Token-RNN-BC improves PV rate relative to Token-BC, but remains far below Spotlight and
two-step DAgger on pass rate.

The failure is mechanistic: on steps where \scalar{obstacle\_pressure} $\ge 0.35$,
Spotlight Reflex enforces a hard reference rule—\token{evasive}$_\text{escape}$ is scored
94 while \token{nudge}$_\text{escape}$ is not offered---committing to full lateral
displacement before force cancellation can occur. Token-BC, trained on expert transitions
in which \token{maintain} appears 91\% of the time, assigns insufficient probability mass
to \token{evasive} in high-pressure states: even with inverse-frequency class weights,
the learned boundary is an approximation that admits errors.
Continuous-BC degrades further: it predicts a scalar lateral offset that can take values
between nudge and evasive, producing trajectories that are geometrically ambiguous under
tight corridor constraints.

This is consistent with the discrete bottleneck mechanism: in the Gauntlet suite, four
obstacles are placed simultaneously with matched seeds. The potential-field
baseline computes a resultant direction from four superposed repulsion vectors. When obstacles
are placed symmetrically or in opposition, the resultant can cancel---a known failure mode
of potential-field planners~\citep{khatib1986potential}. Token-BC inherits a softer version
of the same failure: it \emph{learns} an approximation of when to evade, rather than
\emph{computing} it from clearance geometry. Spotlight Reflex's explicit escape-side
computation prevents this: $\scalar{escape} = \arg\max(\scalar{left\_clr},\, \scalar{right\_clr})$
is always defined and always selects the geometrically correct side.

Two-step DAgger changes the conclusion. Once the learned token policy is trained on states
visited by its own first correction, it recovers most of the escape boundary: on the
held-out LHS sweep it matches Spotlight overall and improves PV rate. This means the
non-interpolation theorem should not be read as saying a neural policy cannot learn the
rule. It says the tokenized action space removes unsafe interpolation paths; the empirical
question is whether the selector reliably chooses the right token. Plain BC does not.
Two-step DAgger mostly does. Three-step DAgger regresses, suggesting that aggregation
quality and weighting become part of the safety frontier.

\subsection{Deconstructing the Aggregation Collapse}
\label{sec:analysis:dagger-collapse}

The three-step regression admits at least three plausible explanations: a takeover by
late recovery states, a class-frequency optimization bias, or a true calibration failure
inside the lightweight token classifier. We tested all three directly.

First, the takeover hypothesis is not supported by the data distribution. The expert BC
pool is 91.10\% \token{maintain}; DAgger-2 and DAgger-3 remain 92.14\% and 91.97\%
\token{maintain} respectively. The token Jensen--Shannon divergence is only 0.00056
(expert vs.\ DAgger-2) and 0.00115 (expert vs.\ DAgger-3). Late DAgger pools therefore
do not replace the nominal manifold; they remain close in first-order token statistics.

Second, class-frequency weighting is not the trivial culprit. Removing inverse-frequency
token weighting raises offline validation accuracy to 96.3\%, but causes a severe
closed-loop collapse: three-step DAgger without class weighting drops to 89.35\% pass
and 2.50\% PV overall, with only 82.50\% pass and 3.75\% PV on Adversarial. This is an
offline/online decoupling effect: the unweighted model fits the dominant nominal token
mass more accurately while under-serving the rare recovery maneuvers needed in closed loop.

Third, source-dependent weighting partially repairs the failure. We assign loss weights
$\alpha=\{1.0, 1.0, 0.5, 0.25\}$ to the expert, first DAgger pool, second DAgger pool,
and third DAgger pool respectively, preserving inverse-frequency token weights. The
result is a large recovery over the naive three-step model: overall pass improves from
92.50\% / 0.46\% PV to 93.06\% / 0.28\% PV, Adversarial recovers from
87.92\% / 0.42\% PV to 89.17\% / 0.00\% PV, and Hidden recovers from
90.83\% / 1.67\% PV to 90.00\% / 0.00\% PV. Yet this still does not exceed the two-step
frontier of 94.54\% / 0.19\% PV.

classifier's boundary. Down-weighting those late pools mitigates the effect, but only
partially, consistent with a calibration problem under limited model capacity rather than
with a pure data-distribution shift.

\subsection{Failure Mode Taxonomy}
\label{sec:analysis:failures}

The 28 Gauntlet non-passes by Spotlight Reflex (Table~\ref{tab:sim_main}; 480 scenarios,
94.2\% pass rate) decompose into three mechanistically distinct classes, each traceable to
a specific scalar interaction rather than to object-label ambiguity:

\paragraph{Over-stopping under cumulative pressure (46\% of failures).}
Multiple background obstacles accumulate \scalar{pressure} individually below the evasion
threshold; combined they exceed the forward-progress reference score. The policy selects
\token{crawl} or \token{stop} when the forward path is geometrically open.
Fix: decompose \scalar{pressure} by source to suppress background accumulation.

\paragraph{Slow re-entry after evasion (30\% of failures).}
\token{evasive\_right} succeeds in lateral separation, but the subsequent
\token{lane\_recover} overlaps temporally with a lingering hazard in the progress zone.
The progress gate penalizes re-entry, causing a stall.
Fix: widen the progress gate temporal window post-evasion.

\paragraph{Synchronized hazard overload (24\% of failures).}
Multiple actors reach closest-point simultaneously: \scalar{left\_clr} $\approx$
\scalar{right\_clr}, so \scalar{escape} is uninformative. Policy defaults to
\token{slow\_yield}; with four simultaneous obstacles, yield is insufficient.
Fix: add a multi-source pressure decomposition to detect symmetric overload.

In all three cases the failure is traceable to a scalar computation, not to an object
label---consistent with the geometry-invariance property. No failure requires knowledge of \emph{what}
the obstacle is; all require knowledge of \emph{where} it is.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{2D abstract simulation.}
The simulator uses 2D Euclidean geometry with kinematic vehicle dynamics. The AlpaSim
diagnostic (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp:alpasim}) shows that internal simulator success is not a
transfer guarantee: the learned selector's calibration can fail when the same token
vocabulary is driven by reconstructed external geometry. The two-clip AlpaSim result is
mechanistic evidence from selection logs, not a population estimate. A full physics
simulation (e.g., CARLA, nuPlan with dynamics) or real-vehicle evaluation is needed before
making broad vehicle-dynamics claims.

\paragraph{Token-count optimality is not established.}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} is a nested removal study, not a search over all token libraries.
The stronger claim that nine is near-minimal requires random token libraries, learned token
sets, parameter perturbations of offsets and speed scales, and larger token counts (e.g.,
12-token variants) to test whether extra classes induce token-flip instability.

\paragraph{Stress-test operating envelope (empirical).}
To quantify the \emph{operating boundary} of geometric reflex vs.\ learned policy, we
swept corridor tightness (wide 9--12~m / medium 6--8~m / narrow 3.6--5~m / extreme
2.2--3.6~m) $\times$ obstacle-actor pressure (1.0$\times$ / 1.5$\times$ / 2.0$\times$
of the Gauntlet baseline), 720 rollouts per cell per agent (25,920 total).
Table~\ref{tab:phase} summarises the PV rates.

and extreme cells push \scalar{left\_clearance} and \scalar{right\_clearance} outside the
training distribution, and the learned boundary degrades. Spotlight Reflex recomputes
escape analytically from the current clearance values and is therefore distribution-shift
invariant by construction.

This phase diagram should be read as an operating-envelope visualization, not as the
primary environment proof. The stronger evidence is the held-out Latin-hypercube sweep in
Sec.~\ref{sec:exp:lhs}, which independently samples generator parameters and includes
recurrent BC plus DAgger-style on-policy correction.

Notably, Token-BC \emph{exceeds} Continuous-BC at extreme OOD (4.4\% vs 6.1--6.4\%):
discrete commitment to a wrong token under distribution shift is more harmful than a
continuous output that partially blends between safe regions.

\paragraph{Temporal scalar history and joint interaction.}
The strongest learned model in this paper is still a token classifier over 10 scalar
features. Token-RNN-BC adds scalar history but remains weak, and interaction-aware
trajectory scorers do not close the stress-suite gap. A richer joint future encoder over
ego and actor rollouts may be needed to distinguish coordinated interaction from static
clearance geometry.

\paragraph{DAgger aggregation is not solved.}
Two-step DAgger is the strongest baseline, but three-step DAgger regresses. This suggests
that blindly adding recovery-state data can distort the training distribution. Future work
should study aggregation schedules, loss weighting by rollout source, validation splits by
closed-loop profile, and safety-constrained selection among DAgger checkpoints.

\paragraph{External calibration is not solved.}
The AlpaSim hard-veto experiment shows that a learned selector can be internally strong and
externally invalid: 99.25\% of DAgger argmax decisions are rejected by the geometric
suppression layer. This is not a threshold-tuning problem; it indicates that proxy-state
reconstruction changes the decision geometry. Future work should validate token policies
under multiple independent simulators, log per-frame disagreement with geometric
constraints, and treat high veto rates as a failure signal rather than as a hyperparameter
to tune away.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================

We have argued that discrete maneuver tokens are useful, but not sufficient. Internally,
tokens remove unsafe interpolation paths, geometric rules provide an interpretable expert,
and two-step DAgger can learn most of that expert's closed-loop boundary on a held-out
Latin-hypercube generator sweep. That internal result alone would support a conventional
methods story: on-policy correction repairs plain BC and nearly matches the geometric
policy.

The AlpaSim diagnostic changes the conclusion. When the learned selector is driven by a
front-camera proxy adapter, its token calibration no longer matches the external geometry.
Lateral clamping repairs action magnitude but not decision validity; hard suppression
recovers some progress but vetoes 99.25\% of learned argmax decisions and collapses the
hybrid almost entirely back to Spotlight-style selection. The useful finding is therefore
not that DAgger plus a veto layer is a winning method. It is that offline accuracy and
internal closed-loop performance can be structurally misleading proxies for external
robustness when the state reconstruction interface changes the geometry of the decision
problem.

The practical implication is that token policies should be audited at the decision level,
not only by aggregate rollout metrics. A learned policy that passes an internal simulator
may still be selecting tokens that an external geometric scorer rejects almost every frame.
For safety-critical imitation learning, per-frame constraint disagreement and external
proxy-state validation should be treated as first-class diagnostics alongside pass rate,
collision rate, and offline validation accuracy.
