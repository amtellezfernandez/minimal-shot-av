\begin{abstract}
We present a transfer result that is negative in the useful sense: in external Waymo
AlpaSim rollouts, collision persists after actor visibility is restored with a
world-frame actor-completion proxy and after the learned selector is bypassed by direct
grid replay. The discovery is not that one policy fails, but that two common sufficiency
stories fail in this matched diagnostic---missing actors alone and learned-selector
ranking alone. The CoRL-facing substrate is the bounded action-candidate interface
evaluated through two external/data-backed surfaces: Waymo AlpaSim transfer diagnostics
and WOD-E2E candidate selection. Waymo AlpaSim validation exposes a non-co-monotone
transfer structure: clamping,
hard vetoing, source decay, world-frame actor completion, actor-axis scoring, and
learned-selector-free direct-grid replay improve different axes but leave collision
largely unresolved. The canonical 30-scene world-frame actor-completion rerun leaves collision unchanged
(0.600 to 0.600), even though offroad, wrong-lane, and progress move. On the same 18
collision clips, learned-selector-free direct-grid replay improves only two paired clips
(1.000 to 0.889, $p=0.5000$). The audits explain why this is not a weak null result but a
localization result. At first impact, baseline logs contain zero structured hazards in all
18 collision clips; same-frame actor-completion reconstructions are actor-positive in all 18,
and the 17 actor-completion collision clips with selector logs all contain structured hazards.
Matched non-collision controls have far lower actor load (median actor-proxy hazard count 4
vs.\ 22, median closest clearance 14.96\,m vs.\ 5.59\,m). Yet once actor visibility is
restored, the same collision surface remains. The diagnostic ladder therefore localizes
the residual boundary to components not isolated by actor completion or learned-selector
removal: candidate feasibility, controller execution, proxy-execution mismatch, or
per-axis safety definitions. Frozen world/VLA priors on
the Waymo E2E candidate-selection track reinforce the same placement thesis: scalar-only
selection reaches 7.695 RFS, generic latent fusion remains weak, a nonlinear Cosmos head
reaches 7.845 RFS, and adding a latent world-prior plus direct selector reaches 7.848 RFS.
Single-surface rollout metrics and offline accuracy are therefore weak transfer indicators
unless geometric disagreement is instrumented at the candidate-ranking interface and
audited on external/data-backed surfaces.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{autonomous driving, imitation learning, DAgger, external transfer,
discrete action space, calibration, ManeuverToken, geometric world-state}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================

Long-tail failure is a central open problem in autonomous driving. Modern end-to-end
policies can perform well on the common driving distribution but degrade on the rare
scenario compositions that matter most: wrong-way actors, construction corridors, and
multi-hazard interactions~\citep{dauner2023parting,hu2023planning}. The dominant response
is scale---more data, more modalities, larger
models~\citep{chitta2022transfuser,brohan2023rt2}. This paper reports a sharper
falsification result: a learned policy can look plausible under one evaluation surface,
but its external collision surface can survive both a world-frame actor-completion proxy and
learned-selector-free direct-grid replay. The question is therefore not only how to make
the state more grounded, but how to determine which transfer boundary remains load-bearing
after actor visibility and learned-selector removal have already been supplied.

\paragraph{Main discovery.}
The external failure is not explained by two natural sufficiency hypotheses. Missing
actors are real: at first impact, baseline logs contain zero structured hazards in all 18
collision clips, while same-frame actor-completion reconstructions are
actor-positive in all 18. But missing actors are not sufficient in this diagnostic: the
matched 30-scene world-frame actor-completion rerun leaves collision unchanged at
0.600 to 0.600. Learned ranking is also not sufficient: on the same 18 collision clips,
learned-selector-free direct-grid replay improves only two paired clips. The magnitude of
the result is this double sufficiency failure: the residual collision surface survives
both actor completion and learned-selector removal.

The central object is not a single pass rate. It is the ordering that a policy induces
over bounded action candidates under a proxy state, and the way that ordering interacts
with candidate feasibility and closed-loop execution. A transfer intervention can repair
route geometry while worsening collision, or repair progress while leaving lane errors
unchanged. Treating these outcomes as one aggregate robustness score hides the mechanism.
We therefore build a diagnostic ladder that systematically tests the easiest explanations:
matched external reruns, first-impact collision audits, selector-side counterfactuals,
learned-selector-free replay, matched non-collision controls, and controlled proxy-state
perturbations.

Discrete maneuver tokens provide the controlled substrate for this study. Their appeal is
topological rather than architectural. In a multi-hazard scene, the safe trajectory set can
split into disconnected components: for example, pass left, pass right, or stop before the
conflict. A continuous regressor moving between components can produce an intermediate
trajectory that belongs to neither component and intersects the unsafe set. A discrete
selector avoids this interpolation path by choosing among maneuver representatives with
explicit clearance margins. Policy uncertainty changes which representative is chosen; it
does not create a new trajectory between representatives.

This makes grounded selection the transfer-critical interface. Recent latent VLA systems
correctly argue that driving should reason in geometry and dynamics rather than in text.
The unresolved step is how a grounded state representation is converted into a concrete
action when the proxy state is incomplete or biased. Our experiments show that this step
cannot be audited by offline accuracy or one aggregate planning score. It must be measured
per axis---safety, route, lane, progress---at the point where candidates are ranked.

We make four contributions (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}):

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Non-interpolation theorem (Sec.~\ref{sec:method:topology}).}
    We state a conditional safety result: if tokens lie inside disconnected safe maneuver
    classes with clearance margin greater than execution noise, discrete selection cannot
    enter the unsafe set by interpolation, while continuous transitions between classes must
    cross it.

  \item \textbf{Geometric World-State Encoding (Sec.~\ref{sec:method:wstate}).}
    Six O(1) scalars derived from obstacle geometry---with no object identity or semantic
    labels---provide sufficient discriminative signal for the trust-region scorer, and satisfy
    a formally stated invariance to object-identity relabelling.

  \item \textbf{Collision survives actor completion and learned-selector-free replay
    (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp:alpasim}).}
    A 10-clip external transfer matrix shows that clamping, source decay, and hard
    geometric vetoes repair different axes and damage others. A matched world-frame
    actor-completion rerun removes the stale-frame confound but remains inconclusive for
    collision causality: full actor completion leaves raw collision unchanged while
    modestly improving progress and changing lane/offroad behavior in opposite directions.
    A first-impact audit and matched-control bridge show systematic structured-hazard
    dropout at the collision-critical frames: baseline impact logs are actor-empty while
    same-frame actor-completion reconstructions are actor-positive and much denser than
    lead-to-terminal-matched non-collision controls. Actor completion therefore changes
    both visibility and action choice on the collision clips, but the residual collision
    surface still lies beyond learned-selector ranking alone.
    An explicit actor-axis proxy is likewise inconclusive as a positive method. Transfer
    failure is therefore not a single scalar degradation, and collision is not explained
    by either missing actors alone or learned-selector ranking alone in this matched
    diagnostic: offroad,
    wrong-lane, collision, progress, and distance-to-ground-truth expose distinct
    mechanisms.

  \item \textbf{Grounded selection evidence from frozen visual/world priors.}
    In the Waymo E2E candidate-selection track, scalar-only selectors plateau at 7.695
    RFS, InternVLA-only grounding does not help, and linear fusion of InternVLA with
    Cosmos remains weak. A nonlinear direct-policy head over frozen Cosmos 64d embeddings
    reaches 7.845 RFS, while a latent world-prior plus direct selector reaches 7.848
    RFS with lower regret to oracle. This turns the VLA comparison into a placement
    result: frozen geometry/world priors help when they participate in the selector that
    ranks bounded candidates, but not when they are appended as generic latent features.
\end{enumerate}

Taken together, these contributions form a diagnostic ladder that tests increasingly
tempting explanations: matched external reruns test actor visibility, first-impact audits
test what the scorer believes at collision time, selector-side counterfactuals test token
availability, and learned-selector-free replay tests ranking sufficiency.
Across the learned-policy variants, the token library, low-level controller, and AlpaSim
scene set are held fixed; clamping, vetoing, source decay, and axis constraints change
only the selection interface. In the WOD grounding ablation, the candidate pool and
validation frames are held fixed while the latent prior and selector head vary. The CoRL
evidence claim is therefore made from AlpaSim and WOD-E2E, not from internal simulator
rollouts.

\section{Related Work}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Long-tail failure in imitation learning.}
\citet{pomerleau1989alvinn} and \citet{ross2011dagger} established covariate shift as the
core failure mode of behavior cloning. \citet{ho2016gail} mitigated it adversarially but
retained a continuous action space. \citet{bansal2018chauffeurnet} synthesised worst-case
training scenarios; \citet{dauner2023parting} showed that rule-based planners outperform
learned ones on nuPlan~\citep{caesar2021nuplan} in many regimes—supporting the view that
structural inductive biases matter more than model scale for long-tail coverage.

\paragraph{Discrete action spaces.}
DQN~\citep{mnih2015human} demonstrated that discretizing the action space enables stable
deep RL where continuous policy gradients fail. Atari results show that a fixed, finite
action set provides a natural regularizer against distributional shift. We apply this
structural idea to the planning setting without RL: ManeuverTokens provide a finite,
semantically grounded action set whose footprints are invariant to scene uncertainty.

\paragraph{Potential-field and reactive planners.}
Potential-field methods~\citep{khatib1986potential} combine attractive (goal) and repulsive
(obstacle) forces to produce continuous navigation directions. Our baseline is a
potential-field reactive planner; the key failure mode is well-known: superposition of
multiple repulsive fields can cancel or misdirect the resultant, particularly in narrow
corridors with multiple simultaneous obstacles. ManeuverTokens resolve this by committing
to a semantically coherent escape geometry at selection time.

\paragraph{Geometric abstraction for planning.}
Conditional imitation learning~\citep{codevilla2018end} uses a route command to condition
a continuous policy. Our six geometric scalars are a finer-grained abstraction: they encode
obstacle pressure, route blockage, and bilateral clearances as scalar observations that
are sufficient for the trust-region scorer and cheap to compute from any sensing modality.

\paragraph{VLA grounding and latent world priors.}
Recent autonomous-driving VLA work argues that direct trajectory generation and textual
chain-of-thought are not enough: latent reasoning must be grounded in geometric and
dynamic priors. LaST-VLA~\citep{luo2026lastvla}, for example, distills 3D geometric
features and video-world-model dynamics into latent tokens and optimizes trajectory-level
metrics with RL. Our study is complementary rather than competitive. We do not train a
large VLA; instead, we test where such priors should enter a transfer-critical driving
stack. The missing variable is not only representation quality; it is placement. In our
WOD ablation, InternVLA-only features and linear Cosmos--InternVLA fusion remain below the
scalar baseline, while a selector-aligned nonlinear Cosmos head and a latent world-prior
plus direct selector produce the best tracked RFS and regret. The link to LaST-VLA is
therefore operational: physical latent grounding matters most when it constrains the
action-ranking operator over bounded candidates, the same stage that fails under AlpaSim
proxy-state transfer. Without that placement test, aggregate planning scores can hide
non-co-monotone failures across collision, route geometry, lane adherence, and progress.

\paragraph{Transfer diagnostics and mechanism localization.}
Most external-transfer evaluations report whether a policy improves under a target
benchmark. Our goal is narrower: identify which interface changes are load-bearing when
the same token policy is moved from a source proxy state to reconstructed external
geometry. This requires paired interventions rather than a single leaderboard row. The
world-frame actor-completion tests actor visibility, first-impact audits test what the scorer
believes at collision time, selector-side counterfactuals test whether a safe token was
available in the logged candidate set, learned-selector-free replay tests whether ranking was the
bottleneck, and matched non-collision controls test whether impact windows differ from the
rest of the scene set. The resulting negative evidence is part of the mechanism claim: if
collision remains after actor completion and selector removal, then the residual transfer
surface lies beyond the learned selector alone.

\paragraph{Simulator transfer and validation scope.}
\citet{khurana2022differentiable} and \citet{shi2024motiondiffuser} study trajectory
prediction in sensor-realistic environments. Our AlpaSim adapter is a transfer diagnostic
that decouples policy logic from sensor processing; it is not a substitute for physics
simulation or real-vehicle validation.

\subsection{Discrete ManeuverToken Space}
\label{sec:method:tokens}

We define nine ManeuverTokens (Table~\ref{tab:tokens}) covering the evaluated
safety-critical response classes of the SE(2) maneuver manifold. Each token is a pair
$(\alpha, \delta)$: a speed scale
$\alpha \in [0,1]$ relative to current ego speed and a signed lateral offset $\delta \in
\mathbb{R}$ (m), realized as a 20-waypoint, 5-second trajectory at 4~Hz via cubic spline.

\paragraph{Coverage for the evaluated hazard family.}
The nine tokens are not claimed to be globally optimal. They are a minimal engineering
instantiation for the hazard family evaluated here: hard stop, cautious forward progress,
nominal progress, yield-without-stopping, bilateral mild avoidance, bilateral strong
avoidance, and post-avoidance recovery.

\begin{proposition}[Token removal induces a maneuver gap]
For the evaluated multi-hazard family, removing any one response class from the 9-token
library eliminates at least one feasible safe response mode: longitudinal hard stop,
cautious progress, mild lateral avoidance, strong lateral avoidance, or recovery. The
resulting controller must either overreact (e.g., full evasion for a mild obstacle),
underreact (e.g., nudge where full evasion is required), or fail to re-enter the lane.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Geometry-Invariance]
Let $T$ be any bijection on object labels leaving obstacle positions unchanged. Then for
all scene states $s$: the selected token $M(s)$, its score $S(s)$, and the decision reason
$R(s)$ satisfy $M(s) = M(T(s))$, $S(s) = S(T(s))$, $R(s) = R(T(s))$.
\end{proposition}

The geometry-invariance proposition follows from the encoding in
Sec.~\ref{sec:method:wstate}: no token score depends on object labels. The implementation
tests this directly by relabelling obstacles and asserting equality of token, score, and
reason outputs.

\subsection{Topological Safety Through Non-Interpolation}
\label{sec:method:topology}

Let $\mathcal{A}$ denote the action space of 5\,s trajectories, and for scene state $s$
let $\mathcal{U}(s) \subset \mathcal{A}$ be the unsafe set (trajectories violating the
clearance constraint). Let $\mathcal{S}(s)=\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{U}(s)$ be the safe
set. In multi-hazard scenes, $\mathcal{S}(s)$ may decompose into disjoint maneuver classes
$\mathcal{S}_1(s),\ldots,\mathcal{S}_K(s)$, such as stop-before-conflict, pass-left, and
pass-right.

We use the trajectory metric
$d(\tau_a,\tau_b)=\max_t \|\tau_a(t)-\tau_b(t)\|_2$ over the 5\,s horizon. A continuous
policy outputs $a_c(s)\in\mathcal{A}$ and is executed with bounded decision or actuation
error $\epsilon$, $\|\epsilon\|\le\eta$. A token policy selects
$\tau_i\in T=\{\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_N\}$ and executes $\tilde{\tau}_i=\tau_i+\epsilon$.

\begin{theorem}[Safety through non-interpolation]
\label{thm:noninterp}
Fix a scene state $s$. Suppose:
\begin{enumerate}
\item each selected token $\tau_i$ lies in a safe maneuver class $\mathcal{S}_k(s)$;
\item each selected token has clearance margin
  $\mathrm{dist}(\tau_i,\mathcal{U}(s))>\eta$;
\item there exist two safe maneuver classes $\mathcal{S}_i(s)$ and $\mathcal{S}_j(s)$ such
  that every continuous path in $\mathcal{A}$ from $\mathcal{S}_i(s)$ to
  $\mathcal{S}_j(s)$ intersects $\mathcal{U}(s)$.
\end{enumerate}
Then every noisy execution of a selected token remains safe, while any continuous
controller that changes between $\mathcal{S}_i(s)$ and $\mathcal{S}_j(s)$ by interpolation
must pass through an unsafe trajectory.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For a token, $\mathrm{dist}(\tau_i,\mathcal{U}(s))>\eta$ implies that every perturbation
with norm at most $\eta$ remains outside $\mathcal{U}(s)$. For the continuous case, let
$\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal{A}$ be any continuous transition from a trajectory in
$\mathcal{S}_i(s)$ to one in $\mathcal{S}_j(s)$. By assumption, $\gamma$ intersects
$\mathcal{U}(s)$, so there exists $t^\star$ with $\gamma(t^\star)\in\mathcal{U}(s)$.
Thus interpolation between disconnected safe maneuver classes necessarily traverses the
unsafe set, whereas token selection jumps between representatives and does not execute the
intermediate path.
\end{proof}

The theorem is conditional: it does not prove that nine tokens are globally optimal, nor
that the specific numeric offsets in Table~\ref{tab:tokens} are unique. It identifies the
property we test empirically: safety comes from selecting margin-separated maneuver
classes, not from continuous interpolation through the action manifold.

\subsection{Metric-Ordering Limits Under Transfer}
\label{sec:method:ordering}

The external diagnostic requires a second formal distinction. Let $z=\phi(x)$ be a
source proxy-state representation and $z'=\phi'(x)$ be a transferred proxy
representation of the same underlying driving scene. For a candidate token $\tau$, define
axis-specific costs $c_j(z',\tau)$ for collision risk, offroad risk, wrong-lane risk,
progress loss, and tracking error, with lower values preferred. A selector intervention is
monotone across axes only if replacing token $\tau_a$ with token $\tau_b$ weakly decreases
all costs $c_j$ under $z'$.

\begin{proposition}[Non-co-monotone transfer axes]
\label{prop:noncomonotone}
If two transfer metrics induce conflicting orderings over the token library, i.e. there
exist tokens $\tau_a,\tau_b$ and axes $p,q$ such that
\[
c_p(z',\tau_a) < c_p(z',\tau_b)
\quad\text{and}\quad
c_q(z',\tau_a) > c_q(z',\tau_b),
\]
then no intervention that switches from $\tau_b$ to $\tau_a$ can be interpreted as a
uniform transfer improvement. It improves axis $p$ while worsening axis $q$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The first inequality states that $\tau_a$ is better than $\tau_b$ on axis $p$; the second
states that $\tau_a$ is worse than $\tau_b$ on axis $q$. Therefore the switch has opposite
sign on the two metrics. A scalar rollout score that reports only the improvement on
$p$, or only an aggregate of $p$ and $q$, can hide this disagreement.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:noncomonotone} is deliberately modest. It does not claim that
transfer failure is unsolvable. It states the condition under which a single intervention
cannot be judged by one scalar success rate. The AlpaSim matrix in
Table~\ref{tab:alpasim_diag} is an empirical instance: clamping, hard vetoes, and
source-decayed DAgger each improve at least one transfer axis while worsening or failing to
move another.

\subsection{Geometric World-State Encoding}
\label{sec:method:wstate}

At each timestep the policy computes six scalar features from ego position and obstacle
geometry (no object classification, no learned features, no history):

\begin{align}
\scalar{pressure}    &= \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{10 - d_{\min}}{10}, 0, 1\right)
    \label{eq:pressure} \\
\scalar{blockage}    &\in [0,1], \quad \text{fraction of forward corridor cross-section occupied}
    \label{eq:blockage} \\
\scalar{blocked}     &\in \{0,1\}, \quad \text{hard stop within braking distance}
    \label{eq:blocked} \\
\scalar{left\_clr}   &= \text{free lateral space left at half vehicle width (m)}
    \label{eq:lclr} \\
\scalar{right\_clr}  &= \text{free lateral space right at half vehicle width (m)}
    \label{eq:rclr} \\
\scalar{escape}      &= \arg\max(\scalar{left\_clr},\;\scalar{right\_clr})
    \label{eq:escape}
\end{align}

All six scalars are $O(1)$ given a sorted obstacle list ($d_\text{min}$ by distance).
The encoding contains no object identity or semantic labels, so geometry-invariance follows
directly. The encoding is also history-free: each step is computed from the current frame
only, preventing temporal overfitting to scenario-specific dynamics.

\subsection{Trust-Region Scoring}
\label{sec:method:scoring}

Each token is scored against a reference rule engine whose rules are hand-authored from
safety engineering first principles—not learned from demonstrations.

\paragraph{Speed-scaled tolerance.}
\begin{equation}
s(v) = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{v - 1.4}{11.0 - 1.4},\; 0,\; 1\right) \times 0.5 + 0.5
\end{equation}
$s(v) \in [0.5, 1.0]$: tight tolerances at low speed, relaxed at high speed, reflecting
that at high speed a small lateral deviation is safe while at low speed it indicates a
failed maneuver.

\paragraph{Trust-region thresholds.} At 3~s horizon: $\pm 1.0 \cdot s(v)$~m lateral,
$\pm 4.0 \cdot s(v)$~m longitudinal. At 5~s: $\pm 1.8 \cdot s(v)$~m, $\pm 7.2 \cdot s(v)$~m.

\paragraph{Token score.} Let $\delta$ be the normalized overshoot beyond the trust region,
and $r$ a reference rule with target score $r.\texttt{score} \in [0, 100]$:
\begin{equation}
\text{score}_t(\tau, r) = \begin{cases} r.\texttt{score} & \delta = 0 \\ \max(r.\texttt{score}
\cdot 0.1^{\delta},\; 4.0) & \delta > 0 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
S(\tau) = 0.5 \cdot \max_r \text{score}_{3\text{s}}(\tau, r) + 0.5 \cdot \max_r
\text{score}_{5\text{s}}(\tau, r) + P(\tau)
\end{equation}
where $P(\tau)$ adds clearance penalties ($-1000$ if clearance $< 0.55$~m) and progress
bonuses (+40 max). The token $\tau^* = \arg\max_\tau S(\tau)$ is executed.

\paragraph{Reference rules.}
Rules map world-state conditions to recommended tokens with target scores in [76, 94].
Key rules: \scalar{pressure}~$< 0.25$ and open corridor $\to$ \token{maintain}~(92);
\scalar{pressure}~$> 0.20$ with preferred escape $\to$ \token{nudge}$_{\text{escape}}$~(91);
\scalar{pressure}~$\ge 0.35$ with preferred escape $\to$
\token{evasive}$_{\text{escape}}$~(94);
\scalar{blocked}~$= 1 \to$ \token{stop}~(82). Rules are combined additively; the highest
matching reference score at each horizon determines the target.

\subsection{AlpaSim Proxy-State Adapter}
\label{sec:method:alpasim}

Waymo's AlpaSim provides sensor-realistic front-camera input, route command, and ego
dynamics. We bridge these observations to the same
geometric scalar interface via four channels:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Route $\to$ lane curvature.} LEFT/STRAIGHT/RIGHT generates a 5-point sigmoid
  spline ($\pm 2.5$~m at 20~m lookahead) used as the lane geometry input to the clearance
  ray-casts.

\item \textbf{Brightness $\to$ visibility augmentation.}
  $\text{vis} = \max(0,(0.35 - \mu_I)/0.35)$, where $\mu_I$ is mean frame brightness.
  Below 35\% brightness, \scalar{pressure} is augmented proportionally.

\item \textbf{Ego dynamics $\to$ risk scalars.}
  $\text{brake} = \max(0,-a/4.0)$; $\text{low\_speed} = \max(0,(1.5-v)/1.5)$.
  These augment the reference rule thresholds.

\item \textbf{Structured hazards $\to$ obstacle primitives.}
  Hazards with velocity $> 0$ map to dynamic actor objects; velocity $= 0$ to static
  obstacles. Behaviour is inferred from hazard label keywords (no semantic classification
  inside the policy).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Evidence boundary.}
We do not report internal simulator metrics as CoRL evidence. The experimental claims
below use only external/data-backed surfaces: AlpaSim transfer diagnostics and WOD-E2E
candidate selection.

\subsection{AlpaSim External Diagnostic}
\label{sec:exp:alpasim}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering

\label{fig:alpasim}

We use AlpaSim as an external diagnostic, not as a statistically powered safety benchmark.
The purpose is to ask whether the learned selector remains calibrated
when its 10-dimensional proxy state is reconstructed from a front-camera, route
command, ego dynamics, and sparse hazard primitives. We evaluate matched 10-clip WOD-E2E
matrices for which all compared policies complete the same AlpaSim launch path. Unless
otherwise stated, AlpaSim numbers below are raw summaries from
\texttt{aggregate/metrics\_unprocessed.parquet}. We keep score-cutoff summaries in the
release artifact as a separate diagnostic view, but do not use them as the primary method
claim because they can hide incidents after AlpaSim has already applied collision/offroad
or distance-to-ground-truth cutoffs.

Table~\ref{tab:alpasim_diag} falsifies the stronger methods claim that one inference-time
patch can convert the learned selector into a robust external policy. The
unmodified two-step DAgger run fails on all major axes: 0.600 collision rate, 0.900
offroad rate, 0.700 wrong-lane rate, and only 0.034 mean progress. Lateral clamping directly repairs geometry,
reducing offroad by 0.400 and wrong-lane by 0.500 while improving distance-to-ground-truth
from 13.86~m to 5.21~m. But the same clamp raises collision rate from 0.600 to 0.700, so
it is not a uniform transfer improvement. Adding axis constraints to the clamped selector
pushes the geometry repair further: offroad falls to 0.200 and distance-to-ground-truth
falls to 2.25~m. Collision still remains 0.700, which isolates the central external
mechanism: route geometry can be repaired without repairing interaction safety.

The hard-veto hybrid repairs a different axis. It improves mean progress from 0.034 to
0.837 and offroad rate from 0.900 to 0.200, but raises collision rate to 0.800, leaves
wrong-lane rate unchanged at 0.700, and worsens distance-to-ground-truth to 22.71~m. Its
selection log shows why this is not evidence for a successful hybrid policy: the veto
fires on 1987/1990 frames (99.85\%, Wilson 95\% CI [99.56, 99.95]), with 1827 vetoes due
to geometric score gap and 160 due to geometric rank. Spotlight-style selection wins 1965
frames, DAgger wins only 2, and there are 16 compromise selections. The hard veto therefore
recovers progress largely by collapsing back to the geometric scorer, not by combining two
well-aligned policies.

The same logs also explain why stronger selector thresholds are the wrong remedy for
AlpaSim safety. The structured-hazard interface is present in the adapter, but in this
10-clip front-camera transfer matrix the axis-constrained and hard-veto runs both record
0/1990 frames with structured hazards or moving actors. The scorer's top candidate is
therefore interpreted as a clear corridor on 87.0\% of axis-constrained frames and
90.7\% of hard-veto frames. This localizes part of the collision failure upstream of token
ranking: route geometry can be clamped or re-ranked, but interaction safety cannot be
fully recovered from candidates scored against an actor-incomplete proxy state.

The world-frame actor-completion probe fixes the earlier stale-frame confound by storing
actors in world coordinates and transforming them into the current rollout ego frame at
inference time. Table~\ref{tab:alpasim_actor_provenance} separates the historical 10-clip
diagnostics from the matched 30-scene raw rerun that we treat as canonical. In that
matched rerun (Table~\ref{tab:alpasim_actor_axis_method}), collision remains unchanged at
0.600 to 0.600, with zero paired improvements and zero paired regressions across 30
scenes. Offroad worsens from 0.207 to 0.310 on the 29 valid comparisons, wrong-lane
improves from 0.241 to 0.172 on the same valid subset, and mean progress increases from
0.170 to 0.184 (bootstrap 95\% CI [0.004, 0.024] for the paired delta). The actor-complete
proxy therefore changes metric ordering, but it does not establish actor-blindness as the
primary cause of the collision failure surface.

Table~\ref{tab:alpasim_collision_surface} distinguishes the proxy question from the
remaining collision question. In the baseline, every raw-collision clip has a selection
log; at the first collision frame, the geometric scorer's top candidate is \token{maintain}
for all 18 clips and the structured-hazard count is zero. In the actor-completion run, 17/18
collision clips have first-impact selector logs. All 17 have an actor-completion proxy hit, a
positive structured-hazard count, a non-\token{maintain} geometric top candidate, and a
non-\token{maintain} selected token. For the logged actor-completion rear impacts, rear-lane hazards
are present in 14/14 cases. Despite this, actor completion collides on the same 18 clips, with
the same first-impact component in 17/18 cases and a median first-impact time shift of
only +0.1\,s. The failure is therefore not explained by actor visibility or learned action
ranking alone in this matched diagnostic.

Table~\ref{tab:alpasim_candidate_counterfactual} pushes the audit one step further without
running new simulations. It injects the same world-frame actor proxy into the baseline
timeline and reconstructs the candidate set at fixed pre-impact leads. The ordinary axis
test remains too permissive for rear interactions: it still marks at least one candidate
safe in 52/54 actionable frames and even in 5/18 first-impact frames. The stricter
actor-axis test is more diagnostic. It finds no actor-axis-safe token at the first-impact
frame in any collision clip. Across the actionable pre-impact frames, it finds 25
actor-axis-safe frames; the baseline-selected token already satisfies the actor-axis test
in 20 of them, while only 5 are selector-side misses. At scene level, only 4/18 clips have
any actor-axis-safe selector miss, 2/18 have no actionable actor-axis-safe token, and
12/18 have actor-axis-safe selections that still collide later. This narrows the residual
collision explanation: selector miss exists, but it is not the dominant explanation in
the current logs.

We then run the missing closed-loop replay directly. Before closed-loop replay, an open-loop
action-space upper-bound audit found proxy-safe direct-grid actions in 962/1027 actionable
pre-impact frames, and the direct-grid cost selected proxy-safe actions in all 962 such
frames. If cost ranking were the main bottleneck, a learned-selector-free closed-loop replay
should therefore collapse the collision rate. Table~\ref{tab:alpasim_direct_grid_replay}
shows that it does not. The cost-ranked direct grid improves progress
(0.167 to 0.246, paired bootstrap 95\% CI [0.044, 0.115]) but still collides on 16/18
clips. A deliberately cost-free max-clearance objective also collides on 16/18 clips,
while improving distance-to-ground-truth (1.41\,m to 0.74\,m, paired bootstrap 95\% CI
[-1.224, -0.189]). The residual collision surface is therefore not explained by the
learned selector alone, nor by the direct-grid cost preferring unsafe candidates. What
remains unresolved is whether the closed-loop controller cannot execute the proxy-safe
trajectories under AlpaSim dynamics, whether receding-horizon replanning erodes the
open-loop clearance, or whether the proxy clearance model is still mismatched to the
AlpaSim collision surface.

Source-decayed DAgger repairs yet another axis: wrong-lane rate improves from 0.700 to
0.400 and progress improves from 0.034 to 0.175, but collision and offroad rates remain
unchanged and distance-to-ground-truth worsens to 27.16~m. Together, these interventions

geometry, safety, progress, and lane adherence are not co-monotone. External transfer
failure must be diagnosed per axis; a single aggregate pass rate would hide the mechanism.

\subsection{WOD-E2E Grounding Placement}
\label{sec:exp:wod_grounding}

The AlpaSim diagnostics identify the failing interface: action selection under
reconstructed geometry. We therefore test whether frozen latent grounding helps when it is
attached to the same interface on the WOD-E2E candidate-selection track. The leverage in
this ablation is that all rows evaluate the same 479 validation frames and candidate pool;
only the grounding signal and selector head change.

Table~\ref{tab:wod_grounding} provides the strongest connection to recent latent VLA
planning work. It does not say that larger latent priors solve transfer by themselves:
InternVLA-only features underperform scalar geometry, and linear Cosmos--InternVLA fusion
does not close the oracle gap. The positive result appears only when the latent prior is
made decision-facing: a nonlinear Cosmos selector reaches 7.845 RFS, and the latent
world-prior plus direct selector reaches 7.848 RFS with the lowest tracked regret. This is
the same placement principle exposed by AlpaSim: grounding needs to act at the bounded
candidate-ranking stage, where externally invalid token choices are made.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{The Multi-Axis Transfer Result}
\label{sec:analysis:orthogonal}

Table~\ref{tab:alpasim_diag} is the central external result. It should not be read as a
leaderboard where the hard-veto variant is ``best'' because it has the highest progress,
or where clamping is ``best'' because it has the lowest distance-to-ground-truth. Those
readings collapse a multi-axis transfer problem into a single scalar. The interventions
separate the axes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Geometry axis.} Lateral clamping reduces offroad and wrong-lane rates and
    improves distance-to-ground-truth, showing that part of the transfer error is action
    magnitude mismatch under reconstructed road geometry. Axis-constrained clamping
    further lowers offroad and distance-to-ground-truth while leaving collision worse
    than raw DAgger, separating route adherence from safety.
  \item \textbf{Progress axis.} Hard geometric vetoes greatly increase progress and
    distance travelled, showing that the DAgger argmax often chooses externally
    non-progressive tokens.
  \item \textbf{Safety axis.} Neither clamping nor hard vetoes reduce collision rate in
    the 10-clip matrix; both increase it. Geometry repair and progress repair are
    therefore not equivalent to safety repair.
  \item \textbf{Actor-completeness axis.} World-frame actor injection is a diagnostic
    factor, not a solved transfer method: the matched 30-scene raw actor-completion
    rerun leaves collision unchanged despite complete actor injection.
  \item \textbf{Actor-axis method axis.} The explicit rear-risk actor-axis proxy increases
    progress and distance travelled, but it leaves raw collision/offroad unchanged and
    worsens wrong-lane. Scaling this variant as a positive method would overstate the
    evidence.
  \item \textbf{Lane-adherence axis.} Source decay improves wrong-lane rate without
    changing collision or offroad rate, showing that aggregation calibration affects a
    different boundary than the geometric veto.
\end{itemize}

This decomposition is the paper's external-transfer claim. The learned policy is not
simply ``bad'' under the external adapter, and the rule-based scorer is not simply
``better.'' Instead, each correction reveals one latent incompatibility between the
candidate-ranking geometry and the external proxy-state interface. A robust transfer evaluation must expose
these axes separately; otherwise an intervention can appear to improve by moving along
one metric while silently degrading another.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Evidence boundary.}
This paper intentionally does not use internal 2D simulator metrics as CoRL evidence.
Simulator pass rates, proximity-violation rates, COMPASS scores, and GIF rollouts are
development artifacts and are excluded from the claims in Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\paragraph{External sample size.}
The AlpaSim matrices are matched diagnostic probes, not population estimates. The 10-clip
and 30-scene reruns are sufficient to falsify the simple claims tested here---that actor
completion or learned-selector removal alone resolves collision---but they do not support
broad safety-rate claims. Larger external reruns with fixed scene lists, paired
statistics, and at-fault/rear-impact separation are needed before making deployment-level
claims.

\paragraph{Proxy-state calibration.}
The policy consumes route, ego dynamics, and structured hazards through an adapter rather
than through a full perception stack. This is useful for localizing the action-selection
boundary, but it leaves perception, tracking, and controller-execution errors only
partially separated. Future work should run the same candidate-interface audits across
multiple external simulators and log per-frame disagreement with geometric constraints.

\paragraph{WOD-E2E scope.}
The WOD-E2E grounding experiment is a validation-frame candidate-selection study. RFS
improvements show that frozen world priors help when attached to the selector, but they do
not establish closed-loop driving quality or hidden-test performance.

\paragraph{External calibration is not solved.}
The AlpaSim hard-veto experiment shows that a learned selector can be externally
miscalibrated: 1987/1990 DAgger argmax decisions are rejected by the geometric
suppression layer. This is not a threshold-tuning problem; it indicates that proxy-state
reconstruction changes the decision geometry. The 10-clip matrix further shows that
interventions are not co-monotone across collision, offroad, wrong-lane, progress, and
tracking error. The actor-aware external result is also limited: even the matched
30-scene world-frame actor-completion rerun leaves collision unchanged while worsening offroad,
improving wrong-lane, and slightly increasing progress. Future work should validate token
policies under multiple independent
external simulators, log per-frame disagreement with geometric constraints, separate rear/at-fault
collisions, and report per-axis failure metrics rather than tuning one aggregate score.

\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================

We have argued that discrete maneuver tokens are useful, but not sufficient. The important
question is not whether a token interface can be made to look strong in a development
harness; it is whether the selected token remains valid when the state is reconstructed
through external geometry and evaluated per failure axis.

The AlpaSim diagnostic changes the conclusion. When the learned selector is driven by a
front-camera proxy adapter, its token calibration no longer matches the external geometry,
and the resulting transfer error is multi-axis. Lateral clamping repairs offroad and
wrong-lane behavior but increases collision rate. Hard geometric suppression repairs
progress and offroad behavior but vetoes 1987/1990 learned argmax decisions, raises
collision rate, and leaves wrong-lane rate unchanged. Source decay improves wrong-lane
rate but does not repair offroad or collision. Actor completion then shows the same
pattern: full actor completion changes offroad, wrong-lane, and progress, but does not
reduce raw collision. Learned-selector-free replay improves only two collision clips. This is the
central discovery: a transfer failure can remain after the proxy is made actor-visible and
after the learned selector is removed. Missing actors and learned ranking errors are real,
but neither is sufficient to explain the residual collision surface. The result is a
double sufficiency failure for the two easiest external-transfer explanations.

The paper's positive contribution is the diagnostic ladder that makes this falsification
defensible. Matched world-frame actor completion tests actor visibility; first-impact
collision audits show what the scorer believes at the collision frame; selector-side
counterfactuals test whether an actor-axis-safe token exists in the logged candidate set;
learned-selector-free replay tests whether learned ranking is the bottleneck; and the
matched non-collision controls show whether the actor-completion signal is specific to
collision-critical frames. Together these controls show why offline accuracy and
single-surface rollout metrics can be structurally misleading transfer indicators.

The practical implication is that token policies should be audited at the decision level
and per failure axis, not only by aggregate rollout metrics. A learned policy that passes
one evaluation surface may still be selecting tokens that an external geometric scorer
rejects almost every frame. For safety-critical imitation learning, per-frame constraint
disagreement, external proxy-state validation, and separate geometry/safety/progress/lane
metrics should be treated as first-class diagnostics alongside pass rate and offline
validation accuracy. In that sense, the negative external result is not a side note; it is
the mechanism result. It shows that grounded state, actor completion, and selector
constraints can all be necessary while still leaving a safety-critical transfer boundary
unresolved.
